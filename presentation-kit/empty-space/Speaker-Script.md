# The Architecture of Empty Space — Speaker Script

10 slides · about 10 minutes · one English tense per slide. Read it aloud twice before presenting.

## 01 · The Architecture of Empty Space

> Good morning, everyone. Look around this room for a moment. What do you see? Most people see walls, a floor, and a ceiling. But we don't live in the walls; we live in the space between them. Every building begins as an empty space, and architects spend their whole careers shaping that emptiness. The walls cost money, but the space is what we actually use. Today, I want to show you why the void, the empty space, matters more than the walls. Each slide follows one English tense, and the small gold marker at the bottom shows where that tense sits in time. We start with the Present Simple, the tense of facts and general truths: architecture shapes space, and space shapes us.

- TARGET TENSE: Present Simple (Subject + V1 / V-s): facts, general truths, permanent principles.
- Target verbs: see, live, begins, spend, cost, is, use, want, matters, follows, shows, sits, start, shapes
- Also used naturally: Present Simple questions and negatives (What do you see? / we don't live).
- Watch out: "Space shape us" ✗ → "Space shapes us" ✓ (third-person -s).
- Delivery tip: Pause after "What do you see?" and really look around the room. Let the silence work — it is empty space too.

## 02 · The Usefulness of Nothing

> Our story starts more than two thousand years ago in China. The philosopher Lao Tzu wrote a short poem about a clay pot. He noticed something that most people missed: potters made the pot from clay, but people used the empty space inside it. He described doors and windows in the same way. Builders cut openings into walls, and those empty openings made the room useful. Lao Tzu never designed a building, but he gave architecture its oldest lesson. This is the Past Simple. We use it for finished actions in the past: wrote, noticed, made, gave.

- TARGET TENSE: Past Simple (V2): finished actions at a finished time in the past.
- Target verbs: wrote, noticed, missed, made, used, described, cut, made, designed (never designed), gave
- Also used naturally: Present Simple (our story starts, we use it).
- Watch out: "He didn't noticed" ✗ → "He didn't notice" ✓ (after did/didn't use V1).
- Delivery tip: Point to the gold shape inside the pot when you say "the empty space inside it."

## 03 · The Eye of the Pantheon

> Now let's travel to Rome, around 125 AD. When Emperor Hadrian's builders finished the Pantheon, they had already done something extraordinary. They had built a concrete dome forty-three meters wide, and they had shaped the interior so perfectly that a sphere of the same size could fit inside it. Before the Pantheon, Roman temples had usually been dark rooms lit only through their doors. Here, the only window is a hole in the roof: the oculus. As the sun moves, a circle of light travels across the walls like a clock. This is the Past Perfect: had plus the past participle. It shows an action that was completed before another moment in the past.

- TARGET TENSE: Past Perfect (had + V3): an action completed before another past moment ("When the builders finished…").
- Target verbs: had (already) done, had built, had shaped, had (usually) been
- Also used naturally: Past Simple (finished), Present Simple (the only window is, the sun moves, light travels).
- Watch out: "had build" ✗ → "had built" ✓ (V3, not V1).
- Delivery tip: Trace the beam of light with your hand as it sweeps across the dome.

## 04 · When Emptiness Became the Hero

> Let's jump to 1748. An Italian architect and surveyor, Giambattista Nolli, had been measuring the streets of Rome for twelve years when he finally published his famous map. He had been drawing the city in a completely new way. Every building was solid black, and every public space was white: the streets, the squares, and even the insides of churches. Look closely, and you can see the Pantheon's round interior as a white circle. In Nolli's map, emptiness became the hero. This is the Past Perfect Continuous: had been plus the -ing form. It shows how long an action continued before a point in the past.

- TARGET TENSE: Past Perfect Continuous (had been + V-ing): duration of an action up to a point in the past.
- Target verbs: had been measuring, had been drawing (on slide: had been mapping)
- Also used naturally: Past Simple (published, was, became), Present Simple (you can see).
- Watch out: "had been draw" ✗ → "had been drawing" ✓.
- Delivery tip: Let the map wipe finish before you say "emptiness became the hero" — the white space appears exactly as you say it.

## 05 · Space That Flows

> Now we are in Barcelona in 1929, at the International Exposition. While many pavilions were displaying products and machines, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe and Lilly Reich were designing a pavilion that was almost empty. Visitors were drifting between free-standing walls of marble, onyx, and glass, and they were moving from inside to outside without ever opening a door. The space was flowing around the walls like water. This is the Past Continuous: was or were plus the -ing form. We use it for actions that were in progress at a time in the past.

- TARGET TENSE: Past Continuous (was/were + V-ing): actions in progress in the past, often side by side with "while".
- Target verbs: were displaying, were designing, were drifting, were moving, was flowing
- Also used naturally: Present Simple (we use it), Present Continuous intro (we are in Barcelona).
- Watch out: "While the visitors walked, the space was flowing" — keep both background actions continuous: "While visitors were walking…".
- Delivery tip: Follow the moving gold dot with your eyes; the audience will copy you.

## 06 · Engineering with Air

> Engineers have learned the same lesson in a different way: empty space can make a structure stronger. The Eiffel Tower has stood in Paris since 1889, and it has become one of the most visited monuments on Earth. Yet it is mostly air. Its iron structure weighs about 7,300 metric tons, but if you melted it and spread it over its square base, the layer would be only about six centimeters thick. Engineers have also hollowed out beams and slabs, because material is only useful where it carries load. This is the Present Perfect: have or has plus the past participle. It connects the past with results we can still see today.

- TARGET TENSE: Present Perfect (have/has + V3): past actions with results that matter now; "since 1889".
- Target verbs: have learned, has stood, has become, have hollowed out
- Also used naturally: Present Simple (it is mostly air, it weighs), second conditional (if you melted…, it would be).
- Watch out: "The tower has stood since 135 years" ✗ → "for 135 years" / "since 1889" ✓.
- Delivery tip: Stop talking for two seconds when the tower disappears and only the thin gold line remains.

## 07 · The Courtyard: A Climate Machine

> Now let's come home, to old Baghdad. For centuries, families lived around an open courtyard, the hosh. At night, cool air sank into the courtyard and filled the rooms. During the hot afternoon, people moved down to the sirdab, a cool basement, while the badgir, a wind catcher on the roof, pushed fresh air down to them. Today, architects and engineers have been rediscovering these ideas. They have been designing courtyards and atriums that pull hot air up and out like a chimney. We call this the stack effect. This is the Present Perfect Continuous: have or has been plus the -ing form. It describes an action that started in the past and is still going on now.

- TARGET TENSE: Present Perfect Continuous (have/has been + V-ing): started in the past and still continuing.
- Target verbs: have been rediscovering, have been designing
- Also used naturally: Past Simple (lived, sank, filled, moved, pushed), Present Simple (we call, it describes).
- Watch out: "Engineers are rediscovering it since 2010" ✗ → "have been rediscovering it since 2010" ✓.
- Delivery tip: Say the Arabic words naturally — hosh, sirdab, badgir, shanasheel. This is our heritage on the screen.

## 08 · Space Shapes the Mind

> Now, let's talk about this room. Right now, as you are listening to me, the space around you is shaping the way you think. In a 2007 study, people in rooms with higher ceilings thought more freely and creatively, while people under lower ceilings focused more on details. At this moment, you are also keeping a certain distance from the person next to you, without even noticing it. The anthropologist Edward T. Hall called this personal space. So the empty space in this room is not doing nothing; it is working on your mind all the time. This is the Present Continuous: am, is, or are plus the -ing form. We use it for actions that are happening right now.

- TARGET TENSE: Present Continuous (am/is/are + V-ing): actions happening at the moment of speaking.
- Target verbs: are listening, is shaping, are keeping, is not doing, is working
- Also used naturally: Past Simple (thought, focused, called), Present Simple (we use it).
- Watch out: "The space shaping you" ✗ → "The space is shaping you" ✓ (keep the auxiliary).
- Delivery tip: Raise your hand slowly toward the ceiling as the gold ceiling line rises on the screen.

## 09 · Zaha Hadid: Space in Motion

> And now, a name that makes us very proud. Zaha Hadid was born here in Baghdad in 1950, and in 2004 she became the first woman to win the Pritzker Prize, the highest honor in architecture. In her buildings, space never stops. Walls bend into floors, floors rise into roofs, and corridors flow like rivers. In the Heydar Aliyev Center in Baku, the ground lifts, folds, and becomes the building itself. Her work proves that empty space does not have to be a box; it can move. This is the Present Simple again, this time to describe how her buildings work and what they always do.

- TARGET TENSE: Present Simple (second use): describing permanent features — how her buildings are and what they do.
- Target verbs: stops, bend, rise, flow, lifts, folds, becomes, proves, does not have
- Also used naturally: Past Simple for biography (was born, became).
- Watch out: "Her work prove" ✗ → "Her work proves" ✓.
- Delivery tip: Smile on "makes us very proud" — the audience will feel it with you.

## 10 · The Future Will Be Built of Space

> So, what will the future look like? As our cities grow, empty space is going to become more valuable than ever. Architects will design towers with gardens in the sky, open ground floors, and courtyards that breathe. Engineers are going to use voids to cool buildings without wasting energy. I believe the best buildings of the future will not be the ones with the most walls, but the ones with the most meaningful space. So, the next time you enter a building, don't look at the walls. Look at the space; someone designed it for you. Thank you. I'll be happy to answer your questions.

- TARGET TENSE: Future: will + V1 (predictions, beliefs, offers) · am/is/are going to + V1 (plans and evidence-based predictions).
- Target verbs: will look, is going to become, will design, are going to use, will not be, 'll be (I'll be happy)
- Also used naturally: Present Simple (cities grow, I believe), imperatives (don't look / look), Past Simple (someone designed).
- Watch out: "will to design" ✗ → "will design" ✓ · "Engineers going to use" ✗ → "are going to use" ✓.
- Delivery tip: When the screen fills with light, slow down. Deliver the last line, then wait three seconds before "Thank you."
